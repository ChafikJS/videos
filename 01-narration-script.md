### Story 1: The Gas Station  `[01:05]`

The first time I saw him, he was standing in aisle three, facing the coolers. I saw him on the security monitor. When I looked up at the actual aisle, there was nobody there.

`[PAUSE 2s]`

My name's Danny. I was twenty-six, and I'd been working overnights at Lone Mesa Fuel for about eight months. It's a four-pump station on a two-lane highway in West Texas, forty miles from anything in either direction. Ten at night to six in the morning, by myself. On a busy night I'd see fifteen customers. Truckers, mostly. Oil field guys. The occasional family that had badly misjudged a road trip.

The job is mostly waiting. You mop. You restock the cooler. You make coffee at two that nobody drinks until four. And you watch the monitor behind the register, because there's nothing else to look at. Sixteen little squares. Pumps, lot, front door, aisles, back hallway, register.

So that first night, I did what anybody would do. I walked over to aisle three. Empty. Checked the restroom. Empty. When I got back to the counter, the square was empty too. I told myself I was tired, and I mostly believed it.

The next night he was back. Same spot, same time, a little after two. Gray sweatshirt, hood down, back to the camera. Standing completely still, the way nobody stands in a store. And this time I didn't walk over. I just watched the screen, and then looked at the aisle, and then looked at the screen. He was only in one of them.

`[PAUSE 2s]`

I told my manager, Gail, and she laughed. She said the camera system was fifteen years old, and when the hard drive gets full it does this thing where old footage bleeds through. Ghost frames, the repair guy called it. Some customer from a week ago, stuck in the machine. She even showed me one, a pickup at pump two that wasn't there. It made sense. Honestly, I was relieved.

Then a ghost frame turned around. Not all the way. On the third night, I watched his head tilt, like he was listening to something. And on the fourth night, he wasn't in aisle three anymore. He was in aisle two.

That's when I started keeping track. Aisle two. Then aisle one. Then the end of the candy rack, eight feet from the register. Every night, one camera closer. Always with his back to the lens. I never once saw his face.

`[PAUSE 2s]`

Around then, a trucker came in for coffee. Older guy, regular, never said much. He paid, and while I was counting his change he nodded at the monitor behind me and said, "Your buddy's been standing there a while."

I turned around. Camera nine, the one aimed at the counter. There I was. There was the trucker. And there was the man in the gray sweatshirt, about three feet behind my left shoulder. I looked. There was nothing behind me but cigarettes. The trucker left his change on the counter. He didn't come back.

`[PAUSE 2.5s]`

That night I finally did what I should have done first. I went into the recordings. And he wasn't in any of them. Every night I'd watched him, the saved footage showed an empty aisle. He was only ever there live.

But at the bottom of the list, there was one more file. It was dated the fourteenth. Three days from then. 2:14 a.m. Already recorded. I played it. In that footage, the counter was empty. No clerk. Just him, in aisle three.

`[PAUSE 2.5s]`

I'm not a brave person. I quit the next morning, the eleventh. Gail was annoyed. She hired a kid named Tyler to cover, and I felt bad enough that I almost warned him. Almost. But what was I going to say?

On the fifteenth, a sheriff's deputy called me. The station had been found that morning with the lights on and the doors unlocked. Register full. Tyler's car still parked out back. No Tyler. They wanted me to come look at the footage, because I knew the system.

I drove out there. I stood behind that counter one more time, and the deputy played the recording from the fourteenth. 2:14 a.m. The counter was empty. And in aisle three, there was a man in a gray sweatshirt, standing with his back to the camera.

The deputy asked if I recognized him. I said no. Then, on the screen, for the first time, the man turned around and looked straight up into the lens.

`[PAUSE 3s]`

It was me. Same face. Same haircut. Same gray sweatshirt I was wearing right then, in that room. The one I'd bought two days earlier.

The deputy looked at the screen. Then he looked at me. And I still don't have an answer for the question he asked next. He said, "Son... where were you on the fourteenth?"

I was home. I was asleep. I think.

### Story 2: The Hospital  `[07:12]`

The call light for room 412 came on at 3:17 in the morning. Room 412 hasn't had a patient in it since 2009. It doesn't even have a bed.

`[PAUSE 2s]`

I'm Renee. I've been a nurse for fourteen years, the last six on night shift at St. Brennan Memorial. It's a small hospital in an old coal town in Pennsylvania, and it's been shrinking for as long as I've worked there. The fourth floor used to have forty beds. Now we staff twelve. The whole east wing is closed off behind a set of fire doors with a chain through the handles.

Nights on a floor like that are quiet. It was me, one other nurse named Deb, and an aide. Seven patients. You do your rounds, you chart, you listen. After enough years you know every sound that building makes.

So when the board at the nurses' station chimed, I looked up expecting 406 or 409. It said 412. East wing. I figured it was a glitch, and I pressed cancel.

It came back four minutes later. I cancelled it again. It kept coming, all night, every few minutes, and I kept silencing it, because I had real patients and real work. Around five, it stopped.

In the morning I put in a ticket. A maintenance guy named Walt came up, and he wasn't even surprised. He said the call system in the east wing was never disconnected, just abandoned, and when the old boiler cycles on, the pipes shake the wiring in the walls. Happens every winter. He checked the log for me. The boiler had kicked on at 3:15. Two minutes before the first call. That was good enough for me.

The next night, at 3:17, the light came on again. And this time, before I could cancel it, the intercom opened. That's what happens when a patient holds the button down. You hear the room.

I heard breathing. Slow, and a little wet, like someone who'd been lying flat too long. Then a man's voice, very quiet, very polite. He said, "Hello? Is someone coming?"

Deb heard it too. She's the one who called security.

`[PAUSE 2.5s]`

A guard named Curtis unlocked the chain, and the three of us walked down the east wing with a flashlight. I could hear our footsteps, and then, a second later, I could hear them again from farther down the hall. Curtis said it was an echo. Nobody argued.

Room 412 was a bare room. No bed. No chair. Dust on the floor so thick we left tracks. But the call cord was still plugged into the wall, hanging there. And the button on the end of it was warm.

`[PAUSE 2s]`

I could've lived with that. What I couldn't live with was what I found when we got back to the station. There was a new patient on my census. Room 412. Admitted that night. Vitals charted every hour. And every entry was signed with my login.

The name on the chart was Halloran, Francis. Age seventy-one. I knew that name. Mr. Halloran was in 406, thirty feet from where I was sitting. Pneumonia, getting better, going home Friday. I went and checked on him. He was asleep, breathing fine.

I scrolled to the bottom of the chart for 412. The last line was a time of death. 3:17 a.m. Dated the following night.

I reported it. IT said it was a duplicate record, a test file somebody forgot to delete. They removed it. I asked to stay with Mr. Halloran the next night anyway, and my supervisor said that was fine.

`[PAUSE 2s]`

Here's the part I think about. At eleven that night, a pipe burst over the west hall. Water through the ceiling in three rooms. The house supervisor made a decision: open the east wing, roll beds down there, just until morning. I was downstairs getting linens when they moved him. Nobody told me which room.

I got back to the floor at 3:10. I ran. The chain was off the doors, and the hall lights were on in the east wing for the first time in years. He was in 412. The call cord was in his hand. He'd been gone for a few minutes. The monitor they'd wheeled in with him had never been plugged in.

They called it at 3:17, because that's when the doctor got there.

`[PAUSE 3s]`

The duplicate chart came back that morning. IT can't explain that. There's one new entry at the bottom, under my name. I didn't write it. It says, "Patient states he has been pressing the button for a long time, and no one came."

I went back and counted the alarms I silenced that first night. There were eleven. The log on his call cord, from the night he died, shows he pressed it eleven times.

### Story 3: The Security Guard  `[13:15]`

Every door in Building C was locked from the outside. I know, because I'm the one who locked them. So whoever keyed the radio at 1:40 that morning was already inside with me.

`[PAUSE 2s]`

Name's Manny. I'm forty-four, and after twenty years of driving a forklift my back quit before I did, so I took a security job. Overnights at a distribution park southwest of Chicago. Four buildings. Mine was Building C: a million square feet of racking, forty feet high, full of patio furniture and dog food and whatever else people order at midnight.

After second shift clocks out at twelve, it's one guard per building. You sit in a booth by the dock office with a wall of camera feeds, and every hour you walk a tour. Twenty-two checkpoints. You tap your wand on each one, and you radio it in to dispatch. "Checkpoint six, clear." It takes fifty minutes.

The guy who trained me was named Dale. He'd had Building C for six years. Two weeks after I started, he radioed dispatch at the end of his shift, said he was done, he was transferring out, and that was the last anybody saw of him. People quit like that in this business. I didn't think about it.

The first strange thing was the radio. 1:40 in the morning, a click. Somebody pressing the talk button and letting go. Then again. I asked dispatch if they were calling me. They said no.

The second thing was the motion sensors. They started tripping in order. Aisle twelve. Aisle thirteen. Aisle fourteen. Something moving down the building at walking speed, in the dark, in the spaces between cameras. Building C has blind spots. Every building does. The racks are too tall. There are whole aisles where the cameras only see the top of the freight.

`[PAUSE 2s]`

I brought it up to the day supervisor, and she pulled up a schedule and turned the screen around. The floor scrubber. It's a robot, about the size of a golf cart, and it runs itself between one and three. It trips the sensors. And the clicks, she said, were trucker radios bleeding over from the interstate.

That night I went looking, and there it was, humming down aisle thirteen with its little orange light. I actually said "evening" to it. I felt like an idiot, and I felt a lot better.

A week later I was on my one-thirty tour, coming up on checkpoint nine, and my radio said, "Checkpoint nine, clear."

It was my voice.

I hadn't said it. I hadn't even touched the wand to the plate yet. I stood there with my hand halfway up. Dispatch came back, "Copy nine." Like nothing was wrong.

`[PAUSE 2.5s]`

It kept happening. Always one checkpoint ahead of me. Ten. Eleven. My voice, my rhythm, even the little cough I do. Like something had been listening to me for weeks and wanted me to know it had me down.

The scrubber was on its charger that night. I checked.

I followed the sensor trips back to where they started, deep in the forties, the worst blind spot in the building. Somebody had been moving pallets. They'd built walls with them, three high, into a kind of room. I squeezed in with my flashlight.

There was a folding chair. A thermos. And a gap between two pallets, right at eye level for somebody sitting down. I sat down. I looked through it. Two hundred yards away, perfectly framed, was the window of my booth. My chair. My coffee.

`[PAUSE 2s]`

On the floor under the chair there was a radio. Company issue. It had a strip of tape on the back with a name in marker. Dale.

That's when the radio in my hand came on. My voice again. It said, "Building C, all secure. Nobody here but me." And then, from somewhere above me, up in the racks, I heard it say the same thing without the radio.

`[PAUSE 3s]`

I didn't run. I walked, fast, the way you walk away from a dog. I went out the dock door, got in my truck, and I drove home. I never called it in. I never went back.

Nobody ever called me about it, either. Not that night. Not that week. I finally called dispatch myself to explain, and the woman on the phone was confused. She said, "Manny, you checked in an hour ago. You've checked in every night."

`[PAUSE 2s]`

That was in March. I work days now, at a hardware store. But every other Friday, a paycheck from the security company still lands in my account. As far as they know, I've never missed a shift.

And I think about Dale, radioing in to say he was transferring out. Nobody saw him leave. They just heard him say so. I looked, by the way. His truck's still in the back lot of Building C.

### Story 4: The Hotel  `[19:23]`

The phone at our front desk has a button for every room in the hotel. Thirty-six rooms, thirty-six buttons. The call that night came in on a line that doesn't have one.

`[PAUSE 2s]`

I'm Tess. I was the night auditor at the Desert Crown Hotel for almost three years. It's in Calder Wells, Nevada, which is a gas station, a bar, and us, on a highway where you can drive an hour without seeing another car. The hotel was built in 1954. Three floors, one elevator, a neon sign that's been missing a letter since before I got there.

Night audit is a paperwork job. Eleven to seven. You close out the day's charges, run the reports, set out the coffee. The loudest thing in the building was the ice machine.

So the phone rang at 2:30, and the display said 313. A man's voice. Older, soft, good manners. He said, "Sorry to trouble you, miss. Could I have a few more towels sent up to 313?" I said of course. I'd hung up before it hit me. Our third floor goes from 301 to 312.

I took the towels up anyway. The hallway ends at room 312 and a linen closet. I left the towels on the closet shelf, mostly so I could say I did something.

He called the next night. He wanted a wake-up call for 4:40. And he asked, "Has my wife checked in yet? She's driving up from Barstow." I told him no one had checked in. He said, "That's all right. She's always late."

`[PAUSE 2s]`

I asked the owner about it. Mr. Abbott's in his seventies, he's owned the place forever, and he just sighed. He said the phone system was older than me, and extension 313 was a house phone in the third-floor linen closet, for housekeeping. And he said the maintenance man, Gus, thought he was funny.

I went up and looked. Sure enough, there was an old beige phone on the closet wall, and the handset was off the hook, swinging a little. I hung it up. Mystery solved. I even told Gus it was a good one. He looked at me like he didn't know what I was talking about.

The calls didn't stop. And the towels were gone from the shelf.

Then I started noticing the elevator. Around 2:30 every night, with nobody in the lobby, it would start up on its own. I'd watch the numbers. Two. Three. It would stop on three, and I'd hear the doors open, and they'd stay open, way too long. Like someone was holding them for somebody.

`[PAUSE 2s]`

There's a photograph in our lobby, by the brochure rack. The hotel on opening day, 1954. One night I actually looked at it. I counted the windows on the third floor. Seven on the east side. I went out to the parking lot at three in the morning and counted again. Six.

The old registration books are in the basement, in boxes. It took me two nights. June 1962. Mr. Walter Pruitt, of Barstow, California. Room 313. Party of two. There's a check-in date. The line for check-out is blank.

`[PAUSE 2s]`

He called that night for his wake-up call, same as always. And I decided to do it. At 4:40 I picked up the phone and dialed 313.

I heard it ring in my ear. And I heard it ring upstairs. Faint, through the ceiling, not from the closet. From past it.

He picked up. He said, "Thank you, miss. Is it morning?" I said yes, almost. He was quiet for a second. Then he said, "It doesn't look like morning. They took my window."

`[PAUSE 3s]`

Mr. Abbott came in when I called him. He didn't sigh this time. He told me his father closed off the end of the third floor in 1971, after a problem with the roof. Cheaper to wall it up than fix it. He'd honestly forgotten there was a room back there. We emptied the linen closet, and Gus took the back wall down with a pry bar.

There was a door behind it. Brass numbers. 313.

`[PAUSE 2.5s]`

The room was small and dark and it smelled like a closed book. One bed, made. A nightstand, with a phone. The window was bricked over from the outside. Nobody in it. No sign anybody had been in it for fifty years.

Except for the bed. Stacked at the foot of it, folded, were six white towels. They had our laundry tags on them. This year's.

He didn't call after that. For about a week, I thought that was the end of it.

`[PAUSE 3s]`

Then one morning, a little before five, a woman came into the lobby. Sixties, maybe. Headscarf, small suitcase, dusty, like she'd been driving all night. She said she was terribly late, and she was meeting her husband. I asked the name. She said Pruitt.

And I heard myself say, "Room 313. Third floor." She smiled and got in the elevator. I watched the numbers. Two. Three. The doors opened, and this time they closed right away.

`[PAUSE 2s]`

She never came down. There was no car in the lot. And on the lobby camera, I'm standing at the desk, talking, and smiling, and pointing at the elevator. There's nobody else in the frame.

I gave my notice that week. People tell me it's a sweet story. He was waiting, and she came. Maybe. But I'm the one who gave her the room number. And I still don't know what I sent up there.

### Story 5: The Delivery Driver  `[26:16]`

The last stop on my route that night was 4 Harrow Creek Road. One small box, signature required. I found out later the county took Harrow Creek Road off the map in 1987.

`[PAUSE 2s]`

My name's Eli. I'm twenty-nine, and I drive a cargo van for a regional courier out of Montrose, Colorado. Night route. Pharmacy totes, machine parts, lab samples, anything that has to be somewhere in the mountains by morning. Route nine. A hundred and eighty miles of two-lane road, switchbacks, and towns with one stoplight.

You get used to it. You learn where the elk cross and where the ice sits. You learn which stretches have cell service, which is almost none of them. Everything runs off the scanner, this handheld that holds your stops and your map and talks to the depot whenever it can find a signal.

That night I finished my last stop at 11:40. I was thinking about a burrito. And the scanner chirped. New stop added. 4 Harrow Creek Road. One parcel. Signature required.

Here's the thing. There was a box in the back of my van I didn't remember loading. Small, plain, brown paper, old-fashioned string. The label was typed. It matched.

I got one bar and called my dispatcher, Shonda. She was half asleep. She said it happens all the time out there, some cabin on an old plat address the system can't place properly, somebody at the depot adds it late and forgets to tell you. She said, "Just follow the scanner, get the signature, go home." It was a reasonable thing to say.

The scanner took me nine miles up the highway and then told me to turn left where there wasn't a road. Except there was. A gap in the trees. A Forest Service gate, standing open, with a chain hanging off it that had rusted through. The dirt past it was smooth. No washouts. No weeds. Like somebody drove it every day.

`[PAUSE 2s]`

The fog started about a mile in. Headlights just made a white wall. On the scanner, the little arrow that was me slid off the last road on the map and kept going, across nothing. Plain gray screen. The distance to my stop said 0.4 miles. I drove for twenty minutes, and it said 0.4 miles.

I would've turned around. I want that on the record. But the road was one lane with a drop on the right, and you can't turn a van around on that. You can only go where it goes.

Then the trees opened up and there was a house. Two stories, white, porch light on. Lights on upstairs. It looked nice, actually. That's what bothered me. Out there, in that fog, it should not have looked nice.

`[PAUSE 2s]`

I got the box and walked up. And I saw the porch. It was covered in packages. Dozens. Stacked along the railing, piled by the door, some of them so old the cardboard had gone soft and gray. Every one I could read said signature required. None of them had been opened.

And past the porch, back in the trees, there were vans. Five or six, nosed into the brush. A new one like mine. An old boxy one with our company's logo from before I worked there. One so far gone there was a tree growing through the hood.

`[PAUSE 2.5s]`

The front door opened. Not all the way. There was nobody in the gap, just the light from inside, warm and yellow. And I heard a floorboard. One step, toward the door, from someone who weighed a lot.

The scanner chirped in my hand. I looked down. It said DELIVERED. And in the signature box, a name was writing itself. Slowly. In neat cursive.

`[PAUSE 3s]`

I put the box on the top step, and I did not look up again. I backed that van down the mountain in the fog with my door open so I could see the edge. It took until sunrise. When I hit the highway, the gate behind me was shut, and the chain was whole.

At the depot, nobody had added a stop. There was no record of the parcel. Shonda didn't remember my call. But the signature was still on my scanner. R. Tolliver.

There's a plaque in our break room. I'd eaten lunch under it for two years without reading it. Ray Tolliver. Driver. Missing since November 1994. Route nine.

The scanner takes a photo for every delivery. Proof it got there. I didn't look at mine for a month. It shows the porch. The box on the step. And in the dark window next to the door, there are faces. Six, maybe seven. Close together, looking out. They're all wearing the uniform.

`[PAUSE 3s]`

They retired route nine after that. I drive days now, in town. But I kept the scanner, and every night at 11:40, it chirps. New stop added. I've never pressed accept.

Last week, though, the screen changed. It doesn't say one parcel anymore. It says one driver.

### Closing  `[32:13]`

Five workers. Five shifts that didn't end the way they were supposed to.

None of it happened. That's the comfortable part. The uncomfortable part is that tonight, right now, there really is somebody alone at a register on an empty highway. Somebody walking a dark hallway with a clipboard. Somebody watching a wall of cameras, hoping every square stays empty.

Most of them will have a quiet night. Most of them.

If you're one of them... check the monitor. Answer the call light. And if a new stop shows up on your route after midnight, don't press accept.

Thanks for listening. Sleep well.
